\section{Purpose}

The surface pond environment is a shallow liquid reactor exposed to atmosphere,
radiation, sediment or mineral surfaces, and changing liquid level. It is
different from a hydrothermal vent and different from volcanic rock pores. The
liquid does not have to be water: the same class should represent an aqueous
pond, a brine, a hydrocarbon pond, or a custom exoplanetary liquid mixture.

The key physical idea is:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\text{shallow pond}
\rightarrow
\text{liquid-level cycles}
\rightarrow
\text{concentration/dilution}
\rightarrow
\text{UV and sediment chemistry}
}
\end{equation}

The first implementation should create the physical environment object. It may
initially use a single well-mixed pond approximation, but the object must be
organized so that hydrodynamic, radiative, and transport structure can be added
without changing the input parser.

\section{Geometry}

The first surface pond model is zero-dimensional: one well-mixed shallow liquid
body above a sediment or mineral surface. A later model should allow a
depth-averaged horizontal velocity field and vertical profiles of radiation,
temperature, and chemistry.

\begin{verbatim}
atmosphere / radiation input
        |
        v
+--------------------------+
| shallow liquid           |
| dissolved molecules      |
+--------------------------+
| sediment / mineral floor |
+--------------------------+
\end{verbatim}

The geometry is defined by:
\begin{equation}
V(t)=A_{\rm pond}h(t),
\end{equation}
where $V(t)$ is liquid volume, $A_{\rm pond}$ is pond area, and $h(t)$ is liquid
level.

\section{Liquid-level cycle}

The simplest liquid-level cycle is periodic:
\begin{equation}
h(t)
=
h_0
+
\Delta h
\cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{\tau_{\rm cyc}}\right),
\end{equation}
with clipping:
\begin{equation}
h(t)\ge h_{\rm min}.
\end{equation}

The variables mean:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $h_0$ is the mean liquid level.
  \item $\Delta h$ is the amplitude of drying/refilling.
  \item $\tau_{\rm cyc}$ is the cycle period.
  \item $h_{\rm min}$ prevents the pond from becoming mathematically negative.
\end{itemize}

\section{Concentration and dilution}

When the pond dries, the same number of molecules occupies less liquid. The
concentration rises. When the pond refills, concentration falls.

If $h_{\rm old}$ is the previous depth and $h_{\rm new}$ is the new depth:
\begin{equation}
C_i^{\rm new}
=
C_i^{\rm old}
\frac{h_{\rm old}}{h_{\rm new}}.
\end{equation}

This is the central surface-pond mechanism. It is why shallow ponds can drive
prebiotic chemistry: second-order and higher-order reactions speed up strongly
when concentrations increase during drying.

\section{Hydrodynamic Layer}

The final surface pond model should not hard-code a single chemical equation at
the start. It should first define the physical transport layer that later
chemical, coacervate, and protocell equations will couple to. The continuum
variables are:
\begin{equation}
\rho,\quad
\eta,\quad
\nu=\eta/\rho,\quad
\kappa_T,\quad
D_i,\quad
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t),\quad
p(\mathbf{x},t),\quad
T(\mathbf{x},t),
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is liquid density, $\eta$ dynamic viscosity, $\nu$ kinematic
viscosity, $\kappa_T$ thermal diffusivity, $D_i$ molecular diffusivity, and
$\mathbf{u}$ the liquid velocity.

For a Newtonian liquid, the reference hydrodynamic model is incompressible
Navier--Stokes with buoyancy and optional interfacial forcing:
\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot \mathbf{u} &= 0,\\
\rho\left(
\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t}
+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}
\right)
&=
-\nabla p
+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf{u}
+\rho \mathbf{g}
+\mathbf{f}_{\rm surf}
+\mathbf{f}_{\rm ext}.
\end{align}
Here $\mathbf{f}_{\rm surf}$ represents surface-tension-gradient forcing
(Marangoni stresses), and $\mathbf{f}_{\rm ext}$ represents later model terms
such as wind stress, rainfall impact, or externally imposed shaking.

For shallow ponds, the first useful reduction is depth-averaged transport:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}
+
\nabla_{\parallel}\cdot(h\mathbf{U})
=
R_{\rm rain}(t)-E_{\rm evap}(t)-Q_{\rm runoff},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{U}(x,y,t)$ is the depth-averaged horizontal velocity. The
zero-dimensional implementation is the special case $\mathbf{U}=0$ and
$h=h(t)$.

\subsection{Convection and internal heat}

If the planet supplies an internal heat flux, or if the surface is heated or
cooled by radiation, the pond may develop buoyancy-driven convection. A first
diagnostic is the Rayleigh number:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Ra}
=
\frac{g\beta\Delta T h^3}{\nu\kappa_T},
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the thermal expansion coefficient and $\Delta T$ the vertical
temperature contrast. For a simple layer, convection begins when
$\mathrm{Ra}$ exceeds a critical value of order $10^3$, with the classical
rigid/free-boundary number depending on boundary conditions. This should be
used as a switch for whether the pond is treated as purely diffusive,
well-mixed, or explicitly convective.

Thermal forcing should therefore be represented by a model object:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}
=
\mathcal{T}
\left(
F_{\lambda}^{\star},
\tau_{\rm atm},
q_{\rm int},
h,
\rho,
c_p,
\kappa_T
\right),
\end{equation}
where $F_{\lambda}^{\star}$ is the stellar spectrum at the planet,
$\tau_{\rm atm}$ atmospheric optical depth, and $q_{\rm int}$ internal heat
flux from the planetary body.

\subsection{Surface tension and evaporation-driven flow}

Surface ponds and droplets can also be moved by gradients in surface tension:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\rm interface}
=
\nabla_s \gamma(T,\mathbf{C}),
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is surface tension and $\nabla_s$ is the surface gradient.
This is the Marangoni mechanism. It matters for evaporating aqueous droplets,
hydrocarbon liquids, and chemically active protocell-like droplets. Evaporation
also creates capillary replenishment flow toward pinned contact lines:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{U}_{\rm evap}
\sim
\frac{J_{\rm evap} L}{\rho h},
\end{equation}
where $J_{\rm evap}$ is evaporative mass flux and $L$ a horizontal length
scale. This transport can concentrate or segregate suspended particles,
polymers, coacervates, and protocell candidates.

\subsection{Generic chemical transport equation}

The chemical equations should be finalized only after the hydrodynamic and
compartment stage is chosen. The general environmental transport form is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C_i}{\partial t}
+
\nabla\cdot(C_i\mathbf{u})
=
\nabla\cdot(D_i\nabla C_i)
+
R_i^{\rm env}(\mathbf{C},T,\mathrm{solvent},F_{\lambda})
+
S_i
-
L_i.
\end{equation}
The current well-mixed equation is the volume-averaged limit of this expression.

\section{Compartment Stage Coupling}

Different protocell-formation stages should not all obey the same equation of
motion. The surface pond environment supplies fields
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u},\quad T,\quad F_{\lambda},\quad h,\quad \rho,\quad \eta,\quad
D_i,\quad A_{\rm reactive},
\end{equation}
and each compartment class uses those fields differently.

\subsection{Dissolved chemicals}

Dissolved species follow advection--diffusion--reaction:
\begin{equation}
\partial_t C_i+\nabla\cdot(C_i\mathbf{u})
=
\nabla\cdot(D_i\nabla C_i)+R_i.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Coacervate or dense liquid droplets}

Coacervates should be treated as deformable dense droplets or particles with
growth, fusion, breakup, and hydrodynamic transport:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{x}_k}{dt}
=
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_k,t)
+
\mathbf{v}_{\rm drift}^{(k)}
+
\sqrt{2D_k}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_k(t),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{dV_k}{dt}
=
J_{\rm in}^{(k)}-J_{\rm out}^{(k)}+R_{\rm phase}^{(k)}.
\end{equation}
The drift term may include buoyancy, sedimentation, thermophoresis,
Marangoni motion, or chemical taxis.

\subsection{Vesicles and protocells}

Vesicles and protocells add a boundary state. Their external motion can still
be advective/diffusive, but their internal chemistry obeys exchange through a
structured interface:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d C_{i,k}^{\rm in}}{dt}
=
R_{i,k}^{\rm in}
+
\frac{A_k}{V_k}
P_{i,k}
\left(
C_i^{\rm ext}(\mathbf{x}_k,t)
-
C_{i,k}^{\rm in}
\right)
-
D_{i,k}^{\rm damage}.
\end{equation}
Thus coacervates, vesicles, and protocells all live inside the same pond
environment, but they do not share the same state equations.

\section{Chemical update}

The minimal concentration equation is:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dC_i}{dt}
=
R_i(\mathbf{C},T,\mathrm{solvent},F_{\lambda},A_{\rm sed})
+
S_i^{\rm atm}
-
k_{i}^{\rm photo}F_{\lambda}C_i
-
k_{\rm wash}C_i
-
\frac{C_i}{h}\frac{dh}{dt}.
\end{equation}

The terms mean:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $R_i$ is production or loss from the chemical reaction network.
  \item $S_i^{\rm atm}$ is atmospheric input or deposition of species $i$.
  \item $k_i^{\rm photo}F_{\lambda}C_i$ is photolysis loss.
  \item $k_{\rm wash}C_i$ is washout or runoff loss.
  \item $-(C_i/h)(dh/dt)$ is concentration during drying or dilution during
  refilling.
\end{itemize}

\section{First implementation target}

The first target is not a full ecological model. It is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item hold a shallow pond state;
  \item update liquid level in time;
  \item concentrate or dilute chemical species as liquid level changes;
  \item add atmospheric source terms;
  \item apply radiation-driven photolysis and washout losses;
  \item expose the state to the chemical reaction network.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hydrodynamics References}

The hydrodynamic layer should be based on established continuum fluid
mechanics and then reduced to the simplest model needed for each environment.
Core references are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Batchelor's \emph{An Introduction to Fluid Dynamics} for incompressible
  flow and transport.
  \item Landau and Lifshitz, \emph{Fluid Mechanics}, for continuum equations,
  interfacial stresses, and transport.
  \item Chandrasekhar's \emph{Hydrodynamic and Hydromagnetic Stability} for
  Rayleigh--Benard convection and instability thresholds.
  \item Pearson's analysis of surface-tension-driven convection for Marangoni
  cells.
  \item Scriven and Sternling for Marangoni effects at fluid interfaces.
  \item Deegan et al. and Hu--Larson for evaporation-driven capillary and
  Marangoni flows in drying droplets.
  \item Zwicker et al. and Hanczyc et al. for chemically active droplets and
  protocell-like self-propelled liquid systems.
\end{itemize}
